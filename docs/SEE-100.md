# SEE-100: Simplified Executive English

*An adaptation of ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English (Issue 8, 2021) for executive communication.*

Version: 1.0

---

## Purpose

ASD-STE100 exists because ambiguity in a maintenance manual kills people. Its rules make each sentence mean exactly one thing to a reader who holds a wrench.

Executive prose rarely fails from that kind of ambiguity. It fails from **evasion**: hedged claims, buried recommendations, passive voice that hides owners, and adjectives where numbers belong.

SEE-100 keeps the machinery of STE — controlled vocabulary, sentence limits, active voice, rigid document structure — but points it at a different target:

> **STE removes ambiguity about actions on objects. SEE removes ambiguity about decisions, owners, numbers, and dates.**

The reader of STE must not misunderstand an instruction. The reader of SEE must not be able to escape a decision.

## How SEE Differs From STE (Design Notes)

| Dimension | STE | SEE |
|---|---|---|
| Failure mode targeted | Misunderstanding | Evasion |
| Domain | Closed (aircraft maintenance) | Open (all business) |
| Dictionary | Whitelist (~900 approved words) | Blacklist + meaning locks (open domain makes a whitelist impossible) |
| Interpretation | Forbidden | Required, but labeled (fact / estimate / opinion) |
| Document order | Sequential (procedures order themselves) | Recommendation first (BLUF — conclusions do not order themselves) |
| Safety instructions | WARNING / CAUTION before the step | Risk statements with severity, trigger, cost, and owner |

# Part 1 — Writing Rules

## Section 1: Words

*(STE Section 1 restricts writers to a dictionary of approved words, one meaning and one part of speech per word. Business is open-domain, so SEE inverts the mechanism: a banned list plus meaning locks. The one-word-one-meaning and one-part-of-speech principles carry over directly.)*

**Rule 1.1** — You can use any plain English word that is not on the banned list (Part 2, Section A).

**Rule 1.2** — Use each word as only one part of speech.
- "Ask" is a verb, not a noun. Not: "the ask" → "the request."
- "Spend" is a verb, not a noun. Not: "the spend" → "the cost."
- "Learn" is a verb. Not: "learnings" → "lessons."
- "Solve" is a verb. Not: "the solve" → "the solution."

**Rule 1.3** — Use meaning-locked words (Part 2, Section B) only with their locked meanings. If you write "blocked," something external must be stopping the work. If you write "on track," the date will hold.

**Rule 1.4** — Do not use jargon when a plain word exists. Jargon is any word your board, your newest hire, and your customer would not all define the same way.

**Rule 1.5** — Use one name for each person, project, and metric, and use it everywhere. Do not call the same project "Phoenix," "the replatform," and "the Q3 initiative" in one document. *(STE Rule 1.11: do not use different technical names for the same item.)*

**Rule 1.6** — Do not turn nouns into verbs. Not: "let's whiteboard it," "can you action this," "we should dialogue." *(STE Rule 1.7: do not use technical names as verbs.)*

**Rule 1.7** — When you must introduce a term of art, define it in one sentence at first use, then use it consistently. *(STE Rule 1.6: unapproved words allowed only as technical names.)*

## Section 2: Noun Clusters

*(Carries over from STE almost unchanged. Noun stacking is how business prose sounds important while saying nothing.)*

**Rule 2.1** — Write noun clusters of no more than three words.
- Not: "enterprise customer churn mitigation workstream kickoff."
- Write: "the project to reduce churn in enterprise accounts."

**Rule 2.2** — When an official name has more than three words, write it in full once, then use a short form. "The Q3 Enterprise Retention Program (the retention program) ..."

**Rule 2.3** — Use articles (the, a, an) and demonstrative adjectives (this, these). Dropping them makes prose read like a slide and forces the reader to reconstruct your grammar.

## Section 3: Verbs

*(STE restricts verb forms to prevent ambiguity. SEE restricts them to prevent evasion. The active-voice rule is the single most important rule in this document.)*

**Rule 3.1** — Use the active voice. Every decision, action, and mistake has a grammatical subject who is a person or a named team. *(STE Rule 3.6: use the active voice.)*
- Not: "The decision was made to sunset the program."
- Write: "Maria decided to sunset the program."
- Not: "Mistakes were made."
- Write: "I shipped the wrong config."

**Rule 3.2** — Use simple tenses only: past ("we shipped"), present ("we ship"), future ("we will ship"). *(STE Rule 3.2.)*

**Rule 3.3** — Do not use helping-verb constructions to soften claims. "Could potentially explore" means "will not do." Write "we will do X," "we will not do X," or "we will decide by DATE." *(STE Rule 3.4: no complex verb structures.)*

**Rule 3.4** — Use a verb to describe an action, not a nominalization. *(STE Rule 3.7.)*
- "Make a decision" → "decide."
- "Conduct an analysis" → "analyze."
- "Provide a recommendation" → "recommend."
- "Achieve alignment" → "agree."

**Rule 3.5** — Do not use phrasal-verb idioms when one verb exists: "circle back" → "discuss on DATE"; "reach out" → "call" or "email"; "touch base" → "meet." *(STE Rule 9.3: no phrasal verbs.)*

## Section 4: Sentences

**Rule 4.1** — Write short sentences. Use a maximum of 25 words per sentence, 20 in asks and action items. *(STE Rules 5.1 and 6.3, same limits.)*

**Rule 4.2** — One idea per sentence. One ask per sentence.

**Rule 4.3** — Do not omit words to sound decisive. "Aligning stakeholders pre-launch" is a fragment, not a plan. Write full sentences. *(STE Rule 4.2.)*

**Rule 4.4** — Use a vertical list when a sentence would contain more than three parallel items. *(STE Rule 4.3.)*

**Rule 4.5** — Use connecting words (but, so, because, therefore) between related sentences. Executives hide causality by deleting it; "because" is the most information-dense word in a status report. *(STE Rule 4.4.)*

## Section 5: Asks and Action Items

*(STE Section 5 governs procedural writing — instructions to a mechanic. The executive equivalent of a procedure is an ask: a sentence that transfers work or requests a decision.)*

**Rule 5.1** — Put the recommendation or ask in the first sentence of the document, before the evidence. A procedure orders itself; an argument does not. Bottom line up front, always.

**Rule 5.2** — Write one ask per sentence, maximum 20 words. *(STE Rules 5.1, 5.2.)*

**Rule 5.3** — Write asks in the imperative or as a direct question with a named recipient.
- Not: "It would be great if someone could look at the pipeline numbers."
- Write: "Dana: review the pipeline numbers and reply by Thursday."

**Rule 5.4** — Every action item names one owner and one date. "We should" is not an owner. "Soon," "next sprint," and "Q3" are not dates.

**Rule 5.5** — Separate information from asks. Notes give context; asks transfer work. Do not hide an ask inside a paragraph of context. *(STE Rule 5.5: notes give information, not instructions.)*

## Section 6: Status and Reports

*(STE Section 6 governs descriptive writing. The executive equivalent is the status update, the board memo, the postmortem.)*

**Rule 6.1** — Give the conclusion first, then the information gradually — each paragraph deepens the story for a reader who chooses to keep reading. The reader who stops after paragraph one still knows the state of the world. *(STE Rule 6.1, inverted for BLUF.)*

**Rule 6.2** — Begin each status with one of exactly three states: **on track**, **at risk**, or **off track**, per the meaning locks in Part 2. No fourth state. "Yellow-ish trending green" is not a state.

**Rule 6.3** — Maximum 25 words per sentence. *(STE Rule 6.3.)*

**Rule 6.4** — One topic per paragraph. Maximum six sentences per paragraph. *(STE Rules 6.5, 6.6.)*

**Rule 6.5** — Report results, not activity. "Held three meetings about churn" is activity. "Churn is 4.1%, down from 4.6%" is a result.

## Section 7: Risk Statements

*(STE Section 7 governs safety instructions: a signal word, a clear command, then the specific risk. Risk statements in business documents get the same structure.)*

**Rule 7.1** — Label each risk with a severity word: **RISK** (could miss the goal) or **BLOCKER** (will miss the goal without intervention). Do not use "concern," "watch item," or "open question" to describe a risk. *(STE Rule 7.1: warning/caution.)*

**Rule 7.2** — State each risk in a fixed form: trigger, consequence, cost, mitigation owner. *(STE Rules 7.2, 7.3.)*
- "RISK: If the vendor misses the API date (Aug 15), launch slips 3 weeks and ~$40k. Mitigation: Priya, fallback scope by Aug 8."

**Rule 7.3** — Place risk statements before the plan they threaten, not in an appendix. A warning after the step is useless in a manual and in a memo. *(S1000D and ATA iSpec 2200 place warnings before the step; STE Section 7 governs their wording.)*

## Section 8: Numbers and Quantification

*(STE Section 8 governs punctuation and word counts — the bookkeeping that makes its limits enforceable. SEE's bookkeeping is numerical: claims are countable or they are opinions.)*

**Rule 8.1** — Every claim of size, speed, or change carries a number, or is labeled an estimate or an opinion. "Significant growth" is banned; "grew 12%" or "my estimate: roughly 10–15%" is allowed.

**Rule 8.2** — Every date is a calendar date. Banned as dates: "soon," "shortly," "in the coming weeks," "next sprint," "by EOQ" without a day.

**Rule 8.3** — Give ranges with a confidence statement, not false precision. "Between $80k and $120k, most likely near $95k" beats "$97,250."

**Rule 8.4** — Name the denominator. "Three customers complained" means nothing without "of the 12 we called" or "of 4,000 active."

**Rule 8.5** — Do not use intensifier adjectives on quantities: "significant," "substantial," "massive," "meaningful," "material" (unless in the accounting sense, meaning-locked). The number carries the weight or the claim is an opinion.

**Rule 8.6** — Compare like with like. Every percentage names its baseline and period. "Up 30%" must say from what, since when.

**Rule 8.7** — You can use all standard punctuation except the semicolon. If the two clauses matter, they deserve two sentences. *(STE Rule 8.1, unchanged — a rare rule that needs no adaptation.)*

## Section 9: Writing Practices

**Rule 9.1** — When a banned word has no one-word substitute, rewrite the sentence. Do not thesaurus-swap; restate the actual thing you mean. *(STE Rule 9.1: use a different construction when word-for-word replacement fails.)*

**Rule 9.2** — Tag judgment. STE forbids interpretation; SEE cannot, because judgment is the product. Instead, every interpretive claim is labeled **fact**, **estimate**, or **opinion** — inline or by section. An unlabeled claim is read as fact and you are accountable for it.

**Rule 9.3** — Use a consistent style for the same construction everywhere: same status labels, same risk format, same date format, same metric names. *(STE Rule 9.4.)*

**Rule 9.4** — Read it back. If a sentence could be quoted back to you in six months and you would want to explain context, the sentence is not finished.

# Part 2 — Dictionary

*(STE Part 2 is a whitelist of ~900 approved words with approved meanings, plus unapproved words with approved substitutions. Business vocabulary is open, so SEE inverts it: Section A bans words and gives substitutions; Section B locks meanings for words that stay.)*

## Section A: Banned Words and Approved Substitutions

| Banned | Write instead |
|---|---|
| leverage (v) | use |
| utilize | use |
| synergy / synergies | (delete; state the specific combined benefit) |
| alignment / aligned | agreement / agreed — name who agreed to what |
| learnings | lessons |
| the ask (n) | the request |
| the spend (n) | the cost |
| the solve (n) | the solution |
| bandwidth (of people) | time |
| headwinds / tailwinds | X fell/rose by N |
| circle back | discuss on DATE |
| reach out | call, email |
| touch base | meet |
| double-click (on a topic) | examine |
| socialize (a document) | send to, discuss with |
| operationalize | put into use; state the mechanism |
| ideate | think, propose |
| action (v) | do, complete |
| dialogue (v) | talk |
| whiteboard (v) | plan, sketch |
| going forward | from now / from DATE |
| at the end of the day | (delete) |
| move the needle | change X by N |
| boil the ocean | (delete; state the smaller scope) |
| low-hanging fruit | the easiest item: NAME IT |
| best-in-class / world-class | (delete, or cite the comparison) |
| robust / seamless / scalable (as praise) | (delete, or state the measured property) |
| pressure-test | check, verify |
| granular | detailed |
| holistic | complete |
| cadence | schedule |
| north star | goal |
| tiger team | team — name the members |
| stakeholders | the people affected: NAME THEM |
| value-add | benefit: state it |
| core competency | strength |
| paradigm shift | (delete; state what changed) |
| deep dive | detailed review |
| pivot | change of plan: state old → new |
| streamline | simplify; state what gets removed |

*This list is a floor, not a ceiling. The test for adding a word: does it let the writer sound certain while committing to nothing?*

## Section B: Meaning Locks

Words that remain approved, but with exactly one meaning. Using a locked word outside its lock is an error, the same as an unapproved meaning in STE.

| Word | Locked meaning |
|---|---|
| **on track** | The committed date and scope will hold. Nothing else. |
| **at risk** | A named trigger could break the date or scope; the risk statement exists (Rule 7.2). |
| **off track** | The committed date or scope will not hold. A new date or scope is proposed in the same document. |
| **blocked** | Work cannot proceed without a named external action. Waiting on yourself is not blocked. |
| **decision** | A choice was made by a named person on a date. "We need a decision" names the decider. |
| **done** | Shipped, delivered, or signed — verifiable by a third party. Not "done pending review." |
| **owner** | The one person answerable for the outcome. An owner is never a team, a committee, or "us." |
| **deadline** | A date with a stated consequence for missing it. A date without a consequence is a target. |
| **target** | A date or number we aim at, with no external consequence if missed. |
| **estimate** | A number the writer derived and stands behind, with stated confidence. |
| **opinion** | A judgment the writer holds; no number claimed. |
| **critical path** | The sequence of dependent tasks that sets minimum completion time. Not "important things." |
| **material** | Accounting sense only: large enough to change a decision. |
| **priority** | An item that displaces other items. More than three concurrent priorities means none exist. |

# Appendix: The Two Standards Side by Side

| STE rule | SEE adaptation |
|---|---|
| 1.1–1.4 Approved words, one meaning, one part of speech | 1.1–1.3 Banned list + meaning locks; one part of speech |
| 1.6–1.7, 1.10–1.11 Technical-name discipline; no slang or jargon as names | 1.4–1.7 Plain words for jargon; one name per project; no noun-verbing; define terms of art |
| 2.1–2.3 Noun clusters ≤ 3 words, use articles | 2.1–2.3 Unchanged in substance |
| 3.4, 3.6, 3.7 No complex verbs; active voice; verbs for actions | 3.1–3.5 Active voice with named actors; no hedging verbs; no nominalizations |
| 4.1–4.4 Short sentences, no omitted words, lists, connectors | 4.1–4.5 Same, plus "because" restored |
| 5.1–5.5 Procedures: 20 words, one instruction, imperative | 5.1–5.5 Asks: BLUF, 20 words, one ask, named owner + date |
| 6.1–6.6 Description: gradual, 25 words, one topic/paragraph | 6.1–6.5 Status: conclusion first, three states, results not activity |
| 7.1–7.3 WARNING/CAUTION + command + risk | 7.1–7.3 RISK/BLOCKER + trigger/consequence/cost/owner |
| 8.1–8.7 Punctuation and word counting | 8.1–8.7 Numbers, dates, denominators, baselines; no semicolon (kept) |
| 9.1–9.4 Rewrite, use words correctly, consistency | 9.1–9.4 Rewrite, tag judgment (fact/estimate/opinion), consistency |
| Part 2 Dictionary (whitelist) | Part 2 Dictionary (blacklist + meaning locks) |
